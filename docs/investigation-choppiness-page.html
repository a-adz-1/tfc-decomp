Smooth graphs, choppy game

Why TFC can look stuttery and feel delayed on a fast modern PC with a high refresh rate, even when every monitoring tool says frame times, pacing and latency are perfect.

Frame-time graphs, frame pacing and "render latency" describe the green boxes: how evenly your PC produces frames. What you see also depends on how those frames meet your monitor's refreshes, and on which moment of the game each frame shows. Both can be uneven while the graph is a flat line.

2How the game paces frames

The engine's frame limiter is actually very precise. While the game window has focus it never sleeps: it loops, checking the clock, and starts the next frame the moment enough time has passed. (It only sleeps, 20–50 ms at a time, when the window is in the background.) So frame times really are even, and your tools are right about that. It also means one CPU core is always busy, which is normal for this engine.

The cap itself: frames may start 1 ÷ (fps_max + 0.5) seconds apart. Without fps_override 1, fps_max can't go above 100.

G-Sync/FreeSync (VRR) fixes this completely: the monitor waits for each frame instead, so every frame stays up exactly 10 ms. Without VRR, the alternatives are a refresh rate that divides evenly into your fps, or a much higher fps (which changes ramp-slide physics, see the main page).

4The extra half frame

Because the cap is fps_max + 0.5, setting fps_max to your refresh rate makes the game produce half a frame per second more than the monitor shows:

  • Vsync on: every 2 seconds one frame has nowhere to go. It waits, the queue grows by a frame, and you get a small hitch plus extra delay, then it repeats.
  • Vsync off: the tear line slowly crawls up or down the screen, a full screen every 2 seconds.

On a 100 Hz screen with the default fps_max 100 this happens out of the box. With VRR, keep fps_max a few below the refresh rate so the cap, not the monitor, sets the pace.

5The game clock correction

This is the part no frame-time tool can see. Your game keeps its own clock of "server time" and draws other players at that time, minus the interpolation delay.

  1. Every update from the server carries the server's time when it was sent.
  2. When it arrives, your game notes how far its own clock is from that time.
  3. Every frame after that, it pulls its clock towards the server's by up to cl_fixtimerate milliseconds (default 7.5), until they are within 1 ms. (If they are far apart, past cl_clockreset, it simply jumps.)

Updates never arrive perfectly evenly: the network adds jitter, and the server sends on its own frames (on the OTFC pickup server every ~10.7 ms, elsewhere 10–15 ms). So the clock is nudged forwards and backwards all the time, and other players are drawn a little ahead or behind where an even clock would put them:

Correction in one framePlayer running (400 u/s)Player after a conc (~1000 u/s)
2 ms0.8 units2 units
7.5 ms (the most per frame)3 units7.5 units, about a quarter of a player's width

At high fps the correction is spread over more frames per second, so it is applied in more, smaller steps, but it is still there. Your own movement isn't affected: you are drawn from your own prediction, not this clock. That's why "I'm smooth but everyone else is jittery" is the typical complaint.

6Other players between updates

Other players only change position when an update arrives, about 93–100 times a second. Your game hides that by showing them ex_interp in the past and blending between the two updates around that moment. The game lets ex_interp go as low as 1000 ÷ cl_updaterate ms (9 ms at 102), but updates arrive 10–15 ms apart, so a very low setting regularly runs out of updates to blend, and players hitch. Keep it at least 0.02; the old default 0.1 is smoothest, and lag compensation means it doesn't cost you hits.

The 25th Anniversary's gl_lowlatency 1 paces your frames to finish just before the monitor needs them (its timing margins default to 2–5 ms), cutting the queue delay. It doesn't change networking.

8Graphics drivers, by vendor

TFC draws with 1990s-style OpenGL (the 25th adds a shader path on top). Modern drivers are built around DirectX 11/12 and Vulkan, and their latency tools (NVIDIA Reflex and Low Latency Mode, AMD Anti-Lag) don't apply to OpenGL games. What does matter:

PlatformWhat to know
NVIDIA, WindowsControl panel, per game: "Vulkan/OpenGL present method" = Prefer layered on DXGI swapchain (modern presentation, VRR in borderless); try "Threaded optimization" Off; "Max frame rate" can replace fps_max as a cap
AMD, WindowsThe OpenGL driver was rewritten in 2022 and is much better since; use a current driver. Anti-Lag doesn't cover OpenGL, so use true fullscreen and gl_lowlatency 1
Intel, WindowsFine on current drivers; use true fullscreen
Linux, AMD/Intel (Mesa)Vsync may be forced by the driver (vblank_mode=0 turns that off); a compositor can add a frame unless the game is fullscreen with direct scanout
Linux, NVIDIA__GL_SYNC_TO_VBLANK=0 for vsync off; __GL_MaxFramesAllowed=1 limits queued frames

These are known platform behaviours, not things we measured. Your results may differ by driver version.

9Why it felt better back then

  • Matched refresh. 72–100 fps (the old caps) on a 72–100 Hz CRT lined up frame for frame.
  • CRTs flash each frame. A CRT lights each frame briefly; an LCD holds it for the whole refresh, which your eye sees as blur when tracking movement. 100 fps on a CRT looked sharper than 100 fps on most LCDs. (High-refresh OLEDs and "strobing" modes get close again.)
  • Full-screen exclusive, no compositor, no vsync by default. Fewer stages between the game and the screen.
  • What was the same: the netcode (rebuilt in 2000 and unchanged since) and the clock correction. Older connections had more jitter, if anything.

11How to test it yourself

  1. Join a server with a friend or bot strafing back and forth in front of you. Turn on net_graph 3.
  2. Watch the strafing player for a minute with the defaults (cl_fixtimerate 7.5).
  3. Set cl_fixtimerate 2 and watch again. If their motion looks steadier, clock correction was part of your choppiness. (We haven't tested this in game yet; tell us what you see.)
  4. Try ex_interp 0.01, then 0.1: the difference shows how much interpolation covers.
  5. Compare windowed and true fullscreen, and vsync on and off.
